A passport is not a number of countries.

The Henley Passport Index — the ranking every newspaper quotes — scores a passport by counting the destinations you can enter without a prior visa. Every destination is worth exactly one point. Kiribati and the United States are worth the same. This project rebuilds that index with destinations weighted by what is actually behind the door, then does the thing rankings almost never do: checks whether the re-weighting changes the answer.

The problem

One point for Kiribati, one point for the United States

Henley & Partners are refreshingly explicit about their method. Using IATA's Timatic database, they score 199 passports against 227 destinations, and "all scores are binary (1 or 0), with no additional weighting or calculations applied." You get a point if you can board without having asked a government's permission first, and nothing if you cannot. In January 2026 that produced Singapore at 192 destinations, Japan and South Korea at 188, and the United States tenth at 179.

Binary counting is a defensible choice, and it makes the index reproducible and hard to fudge. But it embeds two assumptions that nobody would defend if stated out loud. The first is that every destination is worth the same. The second is that every kind of frictionless entry is the same: a visa-on-arrival line in a hot terminal and an automated e-gate in Schengen are both worth one point, while an e-Visa — an online form, a fee, and three days of waiting — is worth zero, the same as being banned.

The rankings that compete with Henley's disagree so sharply that the disagreement is the finding. Singapore is first on Henley, joint second on Arton Capital's Passport Index, and twentieth on the Nomad Passport Index — same world, same passports, three different questions.

That is not a scandal; it is what happens when a weighting scheme is left implicit. A ranking is a claim about what matters, and the flat count is a weighting scheme too — it just never says so. This project's argument is not that Henley is wrong. It is that the weights should be visible, contestable, and tested.

Why anyone should care

Because visa regimes are one of the few remaining forms of inequality that are legally explicit. Neumayer's work on visa restrictions finds that requiring a visa cuts bilateral trade by up to about a fifth and foreign direct investment by up to a third; later gravity-model work puts the hit to travel flows between roughly 50% and 60%. Mau and colleagues describe a widening global mobility divide — liberalization that has been real on average and deeply unequal in its distribution. When the headline instrument for measuring that divide treats a Pacific microstate and a G7 economy as interchangeable units, it flattens exactly the thing it is being used to measure.

The method

Two terms, both made explicit

Every passport index ever published is the same expression: score = Σ credit(passport → destination) × weight(destination). Henley sets credit to a binary and weight to 1. This project varies both and reports what survives.

The whole index, in one line

Both terms are choices. Henley makes them and does not say so; this project makes them, says so, and then varies them.

The friction ladder

Entry regimes are graded rather than dichotomized: visa-free 1.00, visa-on-arrival 0.85, electronic travel authorization 0.70, e-Visa 0.35, visa required 0. These are ordinal judgments, not measurements, so every result is also computed under Henley's own binary rule and under a strict rule where only true visa-free counts. Where a finding holds under one ladder only, it is a finding about the ladder.

What each entry regime is worth

Bars are the graded credit; the orange line is what Henley's binary rule gives the same regime. The gap between them is the modelling choice, and it covers a third of all 39,402 bilateral pairs.

Six pillars from fifteen indicators

Each destination is scored on 15 indicators from the World Bank, UNDP, V-Dem via Our World in Data, and a derived price level — grouped into six pillars: Economy (can you earn and transact there), Development (is it a functioning modern place), Scale (how much world is behind the door), Draw (do people want to go, and can flights get you there), Security (is the access safely usable) and Affordability (how far your money goes). Skewed indicators are logged, winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentile, then min-max scaled; pillars are unweighted means of their members, so weighting happens once, at a level small enough to argue about honestly.

Fifteen indicators, six pillars, one weight per destination

Each pillar is the unweighted mean of its indicators; the percentage is that pillar's share of the Balanced lens. Hover an indicator for what it is doing there.

Five lenses, plus controls

A weight vector is a statement about whose mobility you are measuring, so the project computes several — Balanced, Business, Leisure, Settlement, Raw reach — alongside two weightings nobody chose: one from the first principal component of the destination matrix, one from Shannon entropy. Those two are the control group. If hand-set weights and mechanically-derived weights tell the same story, the story is not an artifact of the analyst. Two more variants need no weighting scheme at all: the share of world GDP and the share of world population you can reach.

Build your own index

The composite is linear in the pillar weights, which means the whole index reduces to six numbers per passport and can be rebuilt in your browser. Move the sliders and the ranking recomputes exactly — this is not an approximation of the pipeline, it is the same arithmetic. Try to find a defensible weighting that reorders the top ten.

Weights are renormalised to sum to 100%, so raising one lowers the rest.

Result 1

Who moves when destinations stop being worth one point each

The biggest single casualty is Malaysia. It sits 6th on the reproduced Henley count — but it shares that score with twelve other passports, so its expected position among the 199 is really 12.0. Weighted, it lands 32nd, an honest fall of 20.0 places. Malaysia's visa-free network is genuinely wide, but it is disproportionately made of small and low-income destinations. Russia falls for the same reason. In the other direction, the gainers are countries with narrow but well-aimed access — Kosovo, Laos, Mongolia, Vietnam — whose comparatively few open doors happen to lead somewhere large. Across all 199 passports the movement is balanced by construction: 92 fall, 84 rise, mean movement 0.00.

Why the movement numbers have a decimal point

Comparing two rankings with different tie structures is harder than it looks, and getting it wrong manufactures findings.

Henley publishes dense ranks, where the next score after a tie takes the next consecutive number — which is why their table can show the United States at rank 10 with 36 passports ahead of it, and why Afghanistan, dead last, appears at rank 97 in a faithful reproduction. Differencing dense ranks against a continuous index is meaningless: the first compresses 199 passports into about 100 ranks and the second spreads them across 199, so 193 of 199 countries "fall" and Afghanistan appears to collapse 74 places while remaining last on both.

Competition ranking — a three-way tie for first is followed by fourth — fixes the scale, and it is what the tables here display. But it does not fix the comparison, because it awards every member of a tie the best position in the group: thirteen passports tied on 160 destinations all become 6th when between them they occupy positions 6 through 18. Since 154 of the 199 passports sit in some tie under Henley's integer scores and almost none do under a continuous weighted score, breaking those ties can only push members downwards. The residual drift is about a rank per passport — small, but always in the same direction.

So movement is measured on fractional ranks, where a tied group sits at the average of the positions it actually occupies. The sum of fractional ranks is n(n+1)/2 whatever the tie structure, so both indices sit on the same total, the mean movement is zero by construction, and what is left is real reordering. Malaysia's expected position goes from 12.0 to 32.0; the largest genuine fall in the table is 20.0 places and the largest genuine rise is 12.5.

The same five passports under all three conventions

Three tie on 160. Only the fractional column sums to n(n+1)/2 — the total a second, tie-free index also sums to — which is what makes it the one safe basis for differencing two rankings.

Result 2

How much of the top of the table is visa-on-arrival?

Of the two modeling choices, the friction ladder turns out to be the bigger lever — not the destination weighting. Holding weights fixed and only changing how entry regimes are scored moves the median passport 4 places, and the most sensitive one 33.5. South Korea and Japan sit at expected positions 2 and 3.5 under Henley's binary rule, and both fall to 35.5 under a strict rule that counts only true visa-free entry — because a large share of their access is visa-on-arrival rather than visa-free. Malaysia moves the opposite way, from 33 to 4.5, because its access is unusually genuinely visa-free.

Result 3 — the uncomfortable one

The weighting barely matters, and that is the most useful thing here

Here is the result that argues against this project's own premise. Across every variant — five hand-set lenses, PCA weights, entropy weights, three levels of weight dispersion — the rankings agree at Kendall's tau above 0.91. Resampling the six pillar weights 3,000 times from a Dirichlet wide enough to cover any defensible weighting moves the median passport by 2 rank. Even stretching the gap between the best and worst destination from 3.5× to 143× only pulls the correlation with the plain count down to 0.91.

Which modeling choices actually change the answer

Kendall's tau-b between every pair of index variants. 1.00 means an identical ordering; hover a cell for the reading.

Why? Because averaging fifteen min-max-scaled indicators into six pillars, and six pillars into one composite, is a variance sink. The resulting destination weights span only 3.5× from the most to the least valuable country — a far narrower claim than the underlying data supports, where GDP per capita alone spans three orders of magnitude. Any composite built this way is closer to a flat count than its authors intend.

The honest conclusion is not "our index is better." It is that arguing about weighting schemes is mostly a distraction, and the choices that do move the answer are the unglamorous ones: how you score an e-Visa, whether you count days, and whether you look outbound or inbound.

Result 4

The divide is not in the weighting. It is in the graph.

Turn the matrix around and the picture stops being subtle. The United States can reach 153 destinations without a prior visa and admits 46 nationalities on the same terms — a figure that matches Henley's own Openness Index, where the US ranks 78th of 199 while sitting 10th on the passport index. Australia runs an even wider gap. At the other end, 13 countries admit more than 190 nationalities and can reach fewer than 70 destinations themselves.

How unequally mobility is distributed

The further a curve sags below the diagonal, the more concentrated that measure of access is among a few passports.

Measured as a Gini coefficient across passports, access is distributed about as unequally as income in a middle-income country: 0.26 under the plain count, 0.25 under the weighted index — and 0.44 when you ask what share of world output each passport opens. Weighting people rather than countries makes it worse, not better, because weak passports are disproportionately held by large populations.

Result 5

What actually predicts a passport

Regressing each passport's attainment on its own country's wealth, human development, population, rule of law, electoral democracy and trade openness explains 74% of the variance. Development and electoral democracy carry almost all of it; GDP per capita adds nothing once HDI is in the model, which is a collinearity result rather than a finding about money — the two have variance inflation factors above 11 and are not separately identified.

Passport strength is mostly national development — and partly diplomacy

The residuals are where the politics lives. The largest over-performers are countries whose mobility far exceeds their fundamentals; the largest under-performers are countries held down by something other than their economy. This is the quantitative version of the observation that a passport is a reputational asset: you cannot buy one by growing, only by being trusted.

Composition

What kind of world does each passport open?

Size is not the only thing that varies. Two passports that reach the same amount of the world can reach completely different parts of it — and the tilt is legible as policy. Russia and China reach disproportionately cheap destinations and disproportionately few safe or developed ones; the United States and United Kingdom are the mirror image, strong on security and development and weak on raw scale because the largest countries on Earth are closed to them.

Every passport has a shape, not just a size

Each cell is the share of the world's total value in that pillar the passport can reach. Colour is how far that sits above (blue) or below (red) the passport's own average — so the colour is the shape and the number is the size.

Left to itself the data builds a development index rather than a size one. The first principal component of the destination matrix — the axis along which destinations differ most — loads heavily on human development, internet penetration and education, and almost not at all on population or surface area. Raw size turns out to be orthogonal to everything else, which is why the Raw reach lens is the one that most disagrees with the others.

Context

What the numbers are attached to

The Emirati climb

The UAE is the strongest performer of the index's twenty-year history, adding 149 visa-free destinations since 2006 and climbing 57 places — the product of a deliberate, sustained program of bilateral waiver negotiation. It tops this project's reproduction too, and unlike most high scorers it holds position across every lens, because its access is broad rather than regionally concentrated.

The American slide is a reciprocity story

The United States has fallen from first in 2006 to tenth in 2026, the third-largest twenty-year decline after Venezuela and Vanuatu. The mechanism visible in this data is not that the US lost access; it is that it never bought more. Admitting 46 nationalities visa-free while reaching 153 destinations leaves nothing to trade with, and waivers are traded.

China is buying reach with openness

China extended unilateral visa-free entry to dozens of countries through 2025 and 2026, adding Sweden in November 2025 and the United Kingdom and Canada from February 2026, and now ranks well above the US on inbound openness. Because China is one of the largest single blocks of weight in any destination-weighted index, each of those waivers moves other countries' scores more than a typical bilateral deal does — the reason several Southeast and Central Asian passports gain under weighting here.

Passports for sale, and the ceiling on them

Citizenship-by-investment programs sell exactly the thing this index measures. In April 2025 the European Court of Justice ruled Malta's scheme unlawful, holding that a member state cannot make nationality — and with it EU citizenship — "a mere commercial transaction." The Caribbean programs that remain sell access to a narrower world, and the weighted index is the natural way to see how much narrower.

The binary is about to get blurrier

The EU's ETIAS authorization, repeatedly delayed and now expected no earlier than 2027, will convert visa-free entry to Europe into an electronic travel authorization for 60-plus nationalities. Under Henley's rule that changes nothing — an ETA still scores 1. Under any graded reading it is a real increase in friction for a very large number of travelers, and it will not show up in the headline number at all.

Honesty

What this cannot tell you

  • It is not Henley's data. Their IATA Timatic feed is licensed and not redistributable. This uses the open passport-index matrix — 199 passports against 199 destinations rather than 227 — so scores are not comparable in level. On the 15 reference points published in Henley's January 2026 report, the reproduction's scores correlate at Spearman 0.79 and its ranks at 0.79, with 12 of 15 landing within two places of the published rank. That is a check on the rule, not a claim to be the same index.
  • The friction ladder is a judgment. 0.85 for visa-on-arrival is a defensible number, not a measured one. Everything is therefore reported under all three ladders.
  • Some data is stale by construction. Tourist arrivals and receipts have a median vintage of 2020 for most countries — the pandemic broke the series and it has not fully recovered. Treated as a structural signal of draw, not a current-year figure.
  • 7% of indicator cells are imputed or hand-sourced, mostly for jurisdictions the multilateral agencies do not report separately. Dropping every poorly-measured destination and rescoring moves the median passport by 1 rank, so the conclusions do not rest on them — but the individual numbers for Vatican City, North Korea and Kosovo should be read as estimates.
  • It is a snapshot. The matrix has no time dimension, so nothing here can speak to trends directly; the twenty-year claims above are Henley's, cited, not reproduced.
  • A visa is not a border. Being legally admissible is not being admitted. Nothing here measures secondary screening, carrier checks, or how a passport is actually treated at a desk.

The reproduction, checked against the published table

Applying Henley's own rule — binary credit, flat weights, dense ranking — to the open matrix should land countries in roughly the tiers Henley publishes, and it does. The scores differ because the destination universe differs; the ordering barely does. The UAE is the one visible disagreement, and it is the passport that moved most between Henley's January and July 2026 releases.
